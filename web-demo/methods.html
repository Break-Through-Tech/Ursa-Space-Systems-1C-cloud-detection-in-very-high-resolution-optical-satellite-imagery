Methods

Four classical methods built from simple rules about how clouds look, and one pretrained deep-learning model. All five get the same crops and are scored against the same hand-checked masks.

Classical methods

RGB only and no training data. Each one gives every pixel a score and calls it cloud above a cutoff.

Brightness + whiteness

2 thresholds
Clouds are bright and colorless: red, green and blue are all high and about equal.
cloud = brightness ≥ b  AND  whiteness ≤ w
How it works

Brightness is the average of R, G and B. Whiteness measures how far the three bands are from their average, so 0 means perfectly grey or white. Both cutoffs are picked together on the validation crops.

Good at

  • Thick, bright cloud
  • Fast and easy to explain

Struggles with

  • White rooftops, sand and snow
  • Thin cloud, which is darker and takes on the ground's color
Zhu & Woodcock (2012), the Fmask paper. Whiteness test from Gómez-Chova et al. (2007).

Dark-channel prior

1 threshold
Clear ground almost always has something dark nearby, like a shadow, a tree or water. Cloud and haze wash that out.
dark channel = darkest of R, G, B in a 15×15 patch
How it works

For every pixel it takes the darkest band, then the darkest value in the surrounding patch. If even that is bright, the area is probably cloud or haze. The cutoff is picked on the validation crops.

Good at

  • Haze and thin cloud
  • Ignoring a single bright pixel next to dark ground

Struggles with

  • Large, evenly bright surfaces
  • Exact cloud edges, since the patch blurs them
He, Sun & Tang (2009), Single image haze removal using dark channel prior, CVPR.

HOT (haze-optimized transform)

1 threshold
Haze and cloud raise blue more than red, compared with clear ground.
HOT = distance above the clear-sky line (blue vs. red)
How it works

On clear ground, blue and red follow a steady straight-line relationship. We fit that line on the pixels marked clear in the validation masks, then score each pixel by how far above the line it sits.

Good at

  • Thin cloud and haze
  • Uses color, not just brightness

Struggles with

  • Noise at high resolution, which usually needs a median filter (not added yet)
  • Surfaces that are naturally blue-ish
Zhang, Guindon & Cihlar (2002), Remote Sensing of Environment 82.

Otsu threshold

no tuning
Let each image pick its own brightness cutoff automatically.
cloud = brightness > Otsu cutoff for this image
How it works

Otsu's method splits an image's brightness histogram into the two groups that are most different from each other. Nothing is tuned, so it shows how far a fully automatic rule gets.

Good at

  • Crops with a clear mix of cloud and ground
  • Needs no labeled data at all

Struggles with

  • Crops that are all cloud or all clear, where it still splits the image in two
  • Bright ground, like the brightness rule
Otsu (1979), IEEE Transactions on Systems, Man, and Cybernetics 9(1).

OmniCloudMask (OCM)

An open-source neural network trained to find cloud across many different satellites. We use it as-is, with no retraining.

Tested by its authors on Sentinel-2, Landsat 8, PlanetScope and Maxar. Our Challenge Advisor pointed us to it.

It labels every pixel as one of four classes. We merge thick and thin cloud into one cloud class:

Clear Thick cloud Thin cloud Cloud shadow
Wright et al. (2025), Remote Sensing of Environment 322. MIT license. Code on GitHub
The catch
  • No near-infrared band. OCM expects red, green and NIR. Our imagery is RGB only, so NIR is filled with zeros.
  • Shrunk to about 10 m per pixel, closer to the 10–50 m imagery it was built for.
  • Smoke gets called cloud. In our wildfire crops OCM often marks smoke as cloud.
Why this matters

Missing NIR and the change in resolution could both make OCM look worse on our imagery than it really is. Testing it with a real NIR band is one of our stretch goals.

How we made the ground truth

No cloud masks exist for this imagery, so we labeled our own in CVAT, two different ways.

Hand-only

Labeled pixel by pixel from a blank mask. Fully independent of every method we score.

OCM-assisted

OCM makes a first draft, then every mask is fixed by hand with a pen tablet. Smoke and unsure areas get their own labels.

Why two ways?

Starting from OCM's draft saves time, but could favor OCM when it's scored. Scoring on both mask sets shows whether that happens.

Keeping the comparison fair

Same masks for everyoneEvery method is scored against the same hand-checked crops.
Tune on validation onlyCutoffs are picked for the best F1 on validation crops and never see the test crops.
Smoke scored separatelySmoke and unsure pixels are left out of the main scores, with a separate smoke check.
Same metricsPer-pixel IoU, precision, recall and F1, plus predicted vs. true cloud cover.
